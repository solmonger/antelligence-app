# Harness v0: one front door that picks the models and tools for each request

Date: 2026-11-02. Code: `antelligence/harness/`, `scripts/harness.py`, tests in `tests/harness/`.

## Why

Eshaan's goal, in his words: "build a harness like hermes or harness2 which can cater swarm of slms, llms, decision
models, etc. and wire things in and call the things needed by itself while improving overtime and beating all scores
in terms of token consumption, accuracy, latency."

So far every test asked one question: is setup X better than setup Y on average? The answer kept coming back "the
reader alone is best" (night run, 2026-11-01). But no single setup won everywhere: the ants are near perfect on word
problems, the swarm and Jev are strong on syllogisms, the reader wins on assignment puzzles. A harness turns that
from a weakness into the point: send each request to whatever wins on its kind, stop as soon as it is sure, and count
the whole bill.

## The four parts

| Part | What it does | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Toolbox | Every worker behind one call. Each call returns an answer and its bill: tokens in and out, seconds, dollars. | `tools.py` |
| Recipes | Small programs over the toolbox: "ask the reader", "ask cheap ants one by one, stop when they agree, else ask the reader", "write a checking program first". The same code runs live and on saved work. | `recipes.py` |
| Scorecard | Every request's answer and bill, compared with "always ask the reader" on accuracy, tokens and time together. | `scorecard.py` |
| Learner and planner | Learns which recipe wins for which kind of request; the planner is that lookup, so choosing is free. A share of live requests runs every tool, so it keeps learning. | `learner.py`, `planner.py` |

Workers in the toolbox today:

- `reader`: the bigger local model (Gemma 4 12B on the Mac) answers once.
- `notes`: the reader's short notes on a question (no answer).
- `ant{i}:{alone,notes,both}`: each of the 8 small ants (4 small models from 4 families x 2 ways of looking).
- `code`: the code checker. A model writes a short program that checks every option against the question's rules by
  trying every case. The program runs in a sandbox. If it crashes, or passes no option or more than one, the model is
  told what went wrong and tries again, twice at most. Eshaan okayed code checks on 2026-11-02 ("if we are willing to
  try different approaches, yes").
- `dm:{jev,d1-3b}:{dm1,read}`: decision models, off unless switched on (Jev costs money).

Recipes in v0 (about 50 counting their settings):

- `reader` (the line to beat), `code`, and each decision model alone.
- `vote:<arm>`: all 8 ants, plain vote.
- `seq:<arm>:m<k>`: ants one at a time, smallest first, stop when the leader is `k` votes clear. With `>reader` or
  `>code` they hand the question up when they can't get sure.
- `code>reader`: the checker first, the reader if it can't settle it.
- `reader+check`: the reader answers, the checker checks; a pick the checker rules out is replaced by its one
  passing option.

## How it learns, in plain words

1. Run every tool once on a set of questions and save every step (a "sweep"). The night run's saved answers are
   already a sweep of 480 questions, so they are imported for free, with each call's real tokens and seconds read
   from the run's caches.
2. Replay every recipe on the saved steps. Each gets a value per question: right or wrong, minus a small charge for
   tokens and time (spending as many tokens as the reader costs 2 points, the same for time; accuracy still matters
   most). These weights are settings.
3. The overall winner is the default. For each kind of request, the best recipe there wins, but its score is pulled
   toward its overall score as if there were 10 more questions, so a kind with a handful of lucky questions can't fool
   it. Kinds with fewer than 8 questions keep the default.
4. The kind of a request comes from a few plain things in its text, never from a label: is the answer a number, does
   it list rules, are the rules about order, are they "all/no/some" statements, and what it asks (could, must,
   cannot, a value).
5. The result is always scored on questions it didn't learn from: a whole held-out set, or k folds.

## Borrowed from proven work

- FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia and Zou, 2023): cascades of models, scored on cached replies before going live.
- RouteLLM (Ong et al., 2024): a learned router, judged against "always use the strong model" on cost and quality.
- Early-stopping self-consistency (Aggarwal et al., 2023, "Let's Sample Step by Step"; Li et al., 2024): stop asking
  once more answers can't change the vote.
- Program-aided answers (PAL, Gao et al., 2022; Logic-LM, Pan et al., 2023): let a program check the rules, and
  feed its errors back.

## The test-set rule

Eshaan, 2026-11-02: "lets look up our test set online based on how big tech evals their platforms. for secrecy
reasons on questionnaire, lets do that last and first build out a base harness. this way neither you, nor the others
can cheat and find answers to the questions."

So the final test set is not picked here. It is the last step, modelled on how big labs evaluate, and kept unseen
until then. Everything in v0 is developed and measured only on:

- code-made puzzles (`antelligence/experiments/fresh_questions.py`), new ones per run, with known answers; and
- the night run's saved answers on such puzzles.

**These are dev numbers. They prove nothing about real questions,** and every report the script prints says so.

## What counts as winning

The scorecard compares the harness with "always ask the reader", question by question:

- "beats it on all three": the accuracy gap's 95% range is above 0, and both tokens and time are lower on average;
- "ties it on accuracy, and is cheaper on both": the range includes 0, tokens and time are both lower;
- otherwise it says which part failed.

It also reports tokens weighted by model size (a token from a 1 GB ant costs less compute than one from a 10 GB
reader), because raw tokens make the small models look dearer than they are.

## Honest limits of v0

- Time is the sum of the steps' own times (one Mac runs one model at a time). Model loading is not counted; the
  importer counts any call over ten times its model's usual time as a load, at the usual time, and says how many.
- The night run asked two calls at a time, so a step's time can include waiting for the other.
- The features that sort requests into kinds were written by hand. They may suit the dev puzzles better than real
  questions; the held-out real test will show it.
- The code checker is not in the saved night run (it didn't exist yet). It is measured only in the live smoke run.
  After the first Mac runs (Nov 2) it is benched by default: with the 12B reader as its coder, most programs ran
  past the 2,000-token limit, at about 6,000 tokens a puzzle. `--code` turns it on. See
  `docs/research/harness-20261102/RESULTS.md`.
- The sandbox guards against mistakes in model-written code (it allows only a few pure modules, no files, no
  network, no environment, with time and memory limits). It is not built to stop a determined attacker.

## Long runs on the Mac

Every model call goes through a patience wrapper: when the drive or the model server stalls (as WD_BLACK did on
2026-11-01), the step waits (15 s, 30 s, 1 min, 2 min, 5 min, 10 min), checks the drive answers again, and asks the
same step again. Finished calls are cached, so a stopped run resumes where it was.
